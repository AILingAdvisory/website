02 Executive Summary: The Assurance Paradox in AI-Enabled Red Teaming

On November 25–27, 2026, CREST, the global non-profit accreditation body for the cybersecurity industry, published landmark empirical research titled AI and the Future of Threat-Led Penetration Testing: Capability, Control and Credible Assurance. Drawing on survey responses from 26 specialist threat-led penetration testing (TLPT) providers across 10 countries and two institutional roundtables centered on financial services, the study exposes a defining structural paradox at the heart of enterprise cyber resilience.

Artificial intelligence has moved decisively out of the lab and into live adversarial engagements: 81% of surveyed TLPT providers now deploy AI beyond initial experimentation, and 65% expect AI to support 40% or more of their TLPT tasks within the next 12 months (Help Net Security). Yet when asked about execution authority in regulated production environments—such as tests conducted under the European Union Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), TIBER-EU, or CBEST—0% of the 11 regulated TLPT providers permit AI to execute testing activities autonomously (FF News; Help Net Security).

This 81%-versus-0% divide highlights an urgent governance challenge for Chief Information Security Officers (CISOs), Business Information Security Officers (BISOs), and financial regulators. As offensive AI capabilities accelerate reconnaissance, threat actor emulation, and custom exploit generation, the bottleneck for credible security assurance is no longer offensive sophistication—it is operational control, client data confidentiality, human validation of AI outputs, and the preservation of uncontaminated cyber resilience metrics.

"TLPT, particularly in production environments, has always been high risk and in need of the strongest assurance. AI is amplifying this and is already changing how it is delivered. Being able to generate a convincing attack does not necessarily mean it represents the right threat or provides credible assurance. Professional judgement, technical competence and clear accountability remain essential." — Nick Benson, CEO of CREST (FF News)

04 Technical Architecture & Delivery Breakdown Across TLPT Phases

Threat-led penetration testing differs fundamentally from standard vulnerability scanning or traditional penetration testing. As outlined in CREST's TLPT-FS framework and G7 Fundamental Elements, TLPT evaluates people, processes, and technology on live production systems—typically unannounced to the internal blue team—across four structured phases: Initiation, Threat Intelligence, Penetration Testing, and Closure.

3.1 Where AI Concentrates Today: Reporting, Reconnaissance, and Emulation

According to the CREST survey, reporting and assessment represents the single most common area of live AI deployment, followed closely by reconnaissance and threat-informed preparation (Help Net Security). In threat intelligence and scenario design, AI accelerates the ingestion of open-source intelligence, dark web telemetry, and historical adversary campaigns, mapping tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) to a specific financial institution's architecture.

Among providers applying AI to threat actor emulation, 86% report improved accuracy and realism (Help Net Security). However, CREST warns of a subtle analytical trap: biases in foundation models and training corpora can inject generic, boilerplate attack patterns into threat assessments, blurring the behavioral distinction between specific threat actors. If a red team emulates a generic LLM composite rather than a credible, sector-specific adversary, the resulting test fails to validate resilience against the threats the institution actually faces.

3.2 AI-Enabled Impersonation and Ethical Boundaries

Modern TLPT routinely tests human verification procedures and incident escalation controls. With commercial voice cloning, synthetic video, and hyper-personalized phishing generation, red teams can now simulate executive impersonation and helpdesk social engineering at unprecedented fidelity (Help Net Security). CREST emphasizes that deploying synthetic media against employees requires explicit justification, pre-engagement authorization, and strict proportionality safeguards to prevent psychological harm, reputational damage, or unintended disruption to targeted individuals.

3.3 Offensive Tooling Supervision and Code Validation

Looking ahead, 88% of providers anticipate greater use of AI-generated offensive tooling (Help Net Security). Yet AI-authored scripts and exploit payloads introduce severe supervision risks in live production environments. Hallucinated API calls, unintended destructive side effects, or poorly scoped lateral movement scripts can trigger outages on critical financial infrastructure. Consequently, 92% of providers identify the ability to validate AI-generated technical output—alongside secure data handling, manual exploitation, and understanding AI failure modes—as an essential practitioner skill over the next 12 months (Help Net Security).

05 The Hidden Metric Distortion: Human Approval Delays vs. Blue Team Resilience

One of the most provocative operational insights surfaced by the CREST report concerns how governance controls themselves can inadvertently skew test outcomes. To prevent uncontained AI behavior in live production systems, providers rely on network restrictions, strict access permissions, real-time monitoring, explicit stop conditions, and human-in-the-loop approval gates (Help Net Security).

However, human approval procedures introduce latency into simulated attack chains. When a red team pauses mid-operation to review an AI-generated exploit, obtain peer sign-off, or request client clearance for an unexpected pivot, the temporal cadence of the simulated attack diverges from a real-world intrusion. That artificial slowdown can distort the assessment of an organization's Mean Time to Detect (MTTD) and Mean Time to Respond (MTTR), potentially making a Security Operations Center (SOC) appear more responsive—or conversely, creating unnatural traffic gaps that confuse behavioral analytics (Help Net Security).

To preserve credible assurance, CREST recommends that providers maintain granular audit logs of all automated actions, practitioner interventions, and approval delays, explicitly documenting how changes to testing conditions affect conclusions about cyber resilience (Help Net Security).

06 Client Data Confidentiality and Third-Party Cloud AI Governance

When penetration testers paste internal network topologies, vulnerability scan outputs, Active Directory structures, or sensitive source code into external cloud-hosted AI services, they create a secondary data exposure vector. Both scope/operational control and client data/confidentiality were ranked among the top three delivery risks by 68% of respondents, while 92% stated that submitting client information to external or cloud-hosted AI services requires explicit client approval (Help Net Security).

Furthermore, 62% of respondents identified autonomous testing actions as requiring explicit client approval (Help Net Security). For enterprise procurement and Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM) teams, this establishes clear contractual due-diligence requirements:

  • Data Residency and Retention: Explicit disclosure of where client telemetry is processed, stored, and whether zero-day retention guarantees are contractually enforced.
  • Model Training Exclusion: Binding prohibition against using client engagement data, findings, or credentials to fine-tune commercial or proprietary AI models.
  • Subprocessor and Integration Visibility: Continuous visibility into the technology vendors, API integrations, permissions, and data flows surrounding AI tools—requiring fresh client approval if a provider alters its AI stack mid-engagement (Help Net Security).

07 Regulatory Alignment: DORA, TIBER-EU, and Financial Services Assurance

The timing of CREST's research is closely tied to mandatory regulatory testing cycles in global financial services. Under the European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) Articles 26 and 27, designated financial entities must undergo intelligence-led TLPT against live production systems at least every three years, typically following the European Central Bank's TIBER-EU framework (XHack DORA TLPT Guide; eBuilder Security). Meanwhile, non-TLPT entities remain subject to annual ICT testing requirements under DORA Articles 24 and 25 (XHack DORA TLPT Guide).

Just two weeks prior to this AI research, on November 14, 2026, CREST published practical DORA TLPT guidance alongside standardized Red Team Test Plan, Red Team Technical Report, and Purple Team Report templates. Integrating AI governance into these standardized templates ensures that regulators and boards can verify exactly where AI assisted intelligence synthesis versus where accredited human practitioners executed and validated production tests.

Notably, CREST also observes that some large financial institutions are building internal AI-augmented testing teams (Help Net Security). As basic offensive automation commoditizes, external accredited providers will differentiate on independent assessment, complex scenario design, threat relevance, and defensible professional judgment—qualities essential for regulatory attestation under DORA, CBEST, and comparable national frameworks.

08 Strategic Action Plan for CISOs, BISOs, and Procurement Teams

With 85% of providers expecting AI-related procurement requirements to increase and 81% anticipating more client disclosure requests over the next 12 months (Help Net Security), enterprise security leaders should immediately update their red-team governance baseline:

09 Mandate Pre-Engagement AI Disclosure: Require every TLPT and penetration testing vendor to declare all AI tools, LLM endpoints, and agentic frameworks before signing Rules of Engagement.

  1. Enforce Zero-Cloud-Leakage Clauses: Prohibit uploading client architecture diagrams, credentials, or findings to external cloud AI models without explicit written authorization and verified zero-retention/no-training terms.

10 Prohibit Unsupervised Autonomous Execution: Maintain a strict human-in-the-loop gate for all live production exploitation, lateral movement, and social engineering payloads.

  1. Separate Approval Latency from Detection Metrics: Instruct red teams to log human-approval pause timestamps so blue-team MTTD/MTTR evaluations remain statistically accurate.

11 Require Peer Review of AI-Generated Tooling: Verify that external providers enforce senior practitioner code review and sandboxing for any AI-generated offensive scripts prior to production deployment.

  1. Establish Synthetic Impersonation Safeguards: Define explicit ethical boundaries, target-group exclusions, and escalation rules before permitting voice cloning or deepfake social engineering simulations.
